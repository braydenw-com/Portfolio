Skip to the research

Designing better focus habits for students tired of fighting their phones.

Role
Product Designer
Team
Brayden, Chris DeMassa, Bess Overton
Duration
10 weeks
Recognition
Indigo Design Award Winner · 2026

Students don’t need another app telling them to focus.

College students already know their phones are distracting. The harder problem is actually doing something about it.

We surveyed 30 college students and found they weren’t asking for stricter restrictions. They wanted help staying motivated, planning their time, and taking breaks without immediately falling back into scrolling.

27/30
struggled with procrastination
21/30
were distracted by social media and their phones
61%
had never used a focus app

So we looked at what was already out there.

We looked at three different approaches to focus.

Forest
Great at blocking distractions.
Finch
Made self-care rewarding.
Focus Friend
Brought accountability into the experience.

Each did something well. We saw an opening between them.

What if focusing felt rewarding instead of restrictive?

Three things kept coming up.

Research gave us three directions to design around.

01

Help me know what to do.

Students were already using calendars and planners to organise their work.

Give focus some structure.

02

Give me a reason to keep going.

Students responded to progress and positive reinforcement far more than punishment.

Reward the behaviour we want to encourage.

03

Help me actually take a break.

Putting your phone down isn’t much help if you pick it back up five minutes later.

Make time away from the screen feel worth it.

One experience, three connected ideas.

Rather than treating focus as a timer, we designed around the whole cycle— planning, focusing, stepping away.

01

Know what’s next.

Calendar

A lightweight planning system brings tasks, focus sessions, and breaks into the same experience. Instead of bouncing between a planner and a focus app, students decide what they need to do and immediately start doing it.

Research said students already plan their time—so we brought the plan into the focus experience.

02

Make progress feel good.

Tap Stickers

Students told us they wanted motivation—not another product punishing them for getting distracted. Tap Stickers turn consistent focus into visible progress through collectible gems, achievements, and rewards.

The goal wasn’t to make productivity a game.

It was to give students a reason to want to come back.

03

Put the phone down.

Break Suggestions

This became one of the most interesting parts of the project. A focus app can block Instagram for 30 minutes and still fail if the first thing someone does on their break is open another app.

So instead of only telling students what not to do, we started suggesting what they could do instead.

  • Take a walk
  • Grab water
  • Stretch
  • Make something
  • Actually step away

The best screen in a focus app might be the one that gets you off the screen.

Then we put it in front of people.

23 participants tested the experience. Rather than validating everything we had made, testing showed us exactly where the product still wasn’t clear enough.

01

“Event” didn’t make sense.

Participants struggled with terminology throughout the interface.

Change: use familiar language like Task or Activity, paired with recognisable icons.

02

Scanning needed more guidance.

Participants weren’t always sure where or how to position their phone.

Change: add visual alignment guides and clearer onboarding.

03

New users needed more help.

Some of the more advanced features weren’t immediately understandable.

Change: introduce contextual guidance the first time someone meets a feature.

Testing didn’t tell us the idea was finished. It told us where to keep designing.

Designing for focus meant designing beyond focus.

During the project we tracked our own screen-time habits. That changed the way I thought about the problem. The challenge wasn’t simply how to stop people getting distracted.

How do we make putting the phone down feel better than picking it back up?

That shift pushed the product past timers and restrictions, toward planning, positive reinforcement, and breaks worth taking.

A focus product shouldn’t rely on willpower alone. It should make the better choice a little easier to make.
