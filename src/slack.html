Slack hides its own state.

Slack has the features people need—but finding them and understanding what they’re doing isn’t always easy.

We evaluated common Slack workflows and tested them with 8 participants. Navigation worked. Two other everyday tasks didn’t.

3/8
successfully retrieved a saved message
2/8
successfully managed notifications

We ran moderated user testing sessions to watch where each of those workflows broke down, and why.

Evaluate → Test → Design → Validate

We started by evaluating 20 Slack workflows against Nielsen’s 10 usability heuristics. Four patterns kept appearing: hidden system states, weak feedback, fragmented navigation, and unnecessary workflow friction.

Those findings shaped the tasks we took into moderated usability testing with 8 participants—then 8 unmoderated sessions once there was something to validate.

Three tasks. Two major failures.

01

Finding your way around

8/8 successful

Participants understood the channel structure immediately. It matched how they expected a workplace tool to behave.

“Ohhh Slack. I feel comfy here.”

Nothing to fix. When the interface met expectations, it disappeared into the background.

02

Finding a saved message

3/8 successful Severity 4/4

Participants couldn’t separate Saved, Pinned, and Later. Most searched several places before giving up or settling.

“There is usually a saved button here. This is terribly confusing.”

The concept wasn’t the problem. Slack’s language was.

03

Managing notifications

2/8 successful Severity 4/4

Controls were spread across menus. Participants couldn’t tell what was active, why, or where to change it.

“I have no idea where to even start with this.”

Before people could change the system, they had to understand it.

So, we changed two things.

Rather than redesigning Slack from scratch, we focused on the two moments where testing showed the biggest breakdowns.

01

Make ownership obvious.

Save for Me. Pin for Everyone.

The existing experience asked people to understand the difference between Saved, Pinned, and Later without clearly communicating who each action was for.

3/8→8/8

In validation testing, all 8 participants successfully completed the redesigned message-retrieval task.

02

Show what Slack is doing.

One place for notifications.

The original experience spread notification controls across multiple locations while giving users very little indication of their current state.

We introduced a persistent notification status and brought the related controls into one place.

2/8→8/8

Every participant who completed the redesigned notification task during validation was able to complete it successfully.

Same problems. New designs. New cohort.

We tested the redesigned experiences with 8 new participants through UserTesting.com.

The goal wasn’t to prove we had created the perfect solution. It was to see whether the same failures still appeared in unmoderated sessions, where no researcher was present to observe or ask follow-up questions.

They didn’t.

37.5% → 100%
Message retrieval
25% → 100%
Notification management, among participants who completed the task

Why does this matter?

Slack is something people can interact with hundreds of times throughout a workweek. Small moments of uncertainty can compound quickly.

Making system state, language, and controls easier to understand could mean less time searching, easier feature discovery, and more confidence using the product.

The interface didn’t need more functionality. It needed to communicate the functionality it already had.

Clear systems create confident users.

The biggest problems we found weren’t caused by people misunderstanding what they wanted to do. They came from Slack failing to communicate where things lived, what was active, and what would happen next.

By making ownership clearer and system state visible, we turned two major points of failure into experiences participants could successfully navigate.

And sometimes, making something easier doesn’t mean adding more.

It means making what’s already there make sense.
